[short pause] The idea behind CLOCK started out quite simply.

I wanted a useful master clock for my own rack.

But I also wanted to avoid needing a separate multiple, a separate divider and another trigger sequencer for fairly common rhythm patches.

So CLOCK grew into a ten-HP module with eight outputs that can either work together or do different jobs. Let me show you.

[short pause] The simplest mode is One Clock.

Here, one master clock drives all eight outputs. I can set the tempo with the encoder, use Tap Tempo, and start or stop the clock directly from the front panel.

For a lot of patches, that already removes the need for a separate clock multiple. I have eight copies of the same timing signal available directly from the module.

[matter-of-fact] And because those outputs come from the same engine, transport, phase and reset behaviour remain consistent across all of them.

[short pause] The next mode is Divider Bank.

Here, the eight outputs provide different divisions of the master clock.

So if I need slower clocks for percussion, envelopes, sequencers or resets, I can get them directly from CLOCK instead of patching through another divider module.

[short pause] The most flexible mode is Independent.

Now each of the eight outputs becomes its own rhythm channel. I can switch between the channels, select one, change its function or timing, and then move to another one while the rest keep running.

[short pause] A channel can simply be a Clock channel.

But the channels do not all have to run at the same rate. They can use different musical rate relationships while still sharing the same master timing.

That makes polymetric and polyrhythmic patches quite straightforward without turning the eight outputs into unrelated free-running clocks.

[short pause] Another channel can run a Euclidean rhythm.

Instead of producing every pulse, it distributes a number of hits across a pattern.

That gives you structured rhythmic patterns very quickly, and they can run alongside normal Clock channels.

[short pause] And another channel can use the gate sequencer.

Here, I can define where gates occur in a pattern rather than simply deriving another clock rate.

So I might have a straight clock on one output, a slower clock on another, a Euclidean pattern on a third and a programmed gate sequence on a fourth. And the remaining outputs are still available.

[short pause] That is really the main idea behind CLOCK.

The eight outputs are not just eight copies of one signal. They can be used as eight related rhythm channels.

Because they share the same timing engine, different rates, pattern lengths and rhythm types can still stay musically connected.

[short pause] In practice, that can save quite a bit of rack space.

Depending on the patch, I may not need a separate clock multiple. I may not need a separate divider. And some simple gate-sequencing jobs can also happen inside CLOCK.

It is not meant to replace every specialised rhythm module. It is simply supposed to cover a useful amount of everyday timing work in one relatively compact module.

And to do that in ten HP.

CLOCK is designed as a through-hole DIY project.

That means it is not as compact internally as an SMD design could be, but it is much easier to solder, inspect and repair.

My goal is that somebody with normal DIY soldering experience should be able to build it without specialised equipment.
